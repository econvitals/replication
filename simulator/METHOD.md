# How the runs are made

This is the construction behind every stored run, in words. The code is the final word: almost everything below is in `engine/build.py` (`make_model`, `baseline_frame`, `apply_shock`, `config_task`), with the tariff in `engine/tariff.py` and the business-investment model in `engine/invest_model.py`. Each module’s opening comment and the comments beside the code go into more detail than this file.

## The model and the baseline

All runs use FRB/US, the Federal Reserve Board staff’s large-scale model of the U.S. economy, through the Board’s own Python platform (pyfrbus 1.1.1) and its data package (`LONGBASE.TXT`), both bundled in `fed/`.

* **Baseline.** The simulator’s runs start from the Board’s long-run extrapolation at 2040Q1, a steady state: output at potential, inflation at the 2 percent target and the federal funds rate at the neutral real rate plus the target. The Board’s own demonstration programs start there too. pyfrbus’s `init_trac` gives every equation the tracking residual that makes the model reproduce this baseline exactly; the shock is then applied on top, and what is stored is the simulation minus the baseline. So nothing stored is a forecast: every number is a difference from a steady state.
* **Expectations.** Backward-looking (`var`) is the Board’s default: expectations are forecasts from small vector autoregressions. Forward-looking (`mce`) is `Frbus(..., mce="mcap+wp")`: expectations in financial markets and in wage and price setting are model-consistent, and everything else stays backward-looking. Every forward-looking run follows one set of rules: it is solved 200 quarters (50 years) out, and the policy rule’s neutral real rate r* learns from the real funds rate (`drstar = 1`: r* moves 5 percent of the way toward the real funds rate each quarter). Backward-looking runs keep the Board’s fixed r* (`drstar = 0`).
* **Solution windows.** Backward-looking runs are solved over 45 quarters (2040Q1 to 2051Q1), forward-looking runs over 200 quarters (2040Q1 to 2089Q4), because forward-looking agents need room past the horizon shown: long-term interest rates and stock prices look decades ahead. The first 41 quarters (quarter 0, when the shock starts, to quarter 40) are stored.
* **Solver.** pyfrbus’s Newton solver. A backward-looking solve that fails is retried with its plain Newton option. A forward-looking solve is given 40 iterations; a run that has not converged by then is recorded as not solved rather than retried (see “What did not solve,” below).

## The settings each run combines

* **How the Fed responds.** The funds-rate equation (`rffrule`) is replaced by `dmpex*rfffix + (1-dmpex)*RULE`, where RULE is one of four rules: the Board’s inertial rule (`rffintay`), the balanced-approach rule (`rfftay`), the Taylor (1993) rule and the first-difference rule, the last two written out from the definitions in the Board’s Monetary Policy Report. The three holds keep the funds rate at its baseline for one, two or three years (`dmpex = 1`) and then follow the inertial rule. A hold, or the Fed’s own move, sets `rfffix` net of the two funds-rate tracking residuals (`rff_trac` and `rffrule_trac`), because pyfrbus adds each equation’s residual to its right-hand side; without that the rate would miss its target by up to 0.3 point.
* **The lower bound.** In “at the lower bound” runs the floor `rffmin` is set to the rate the baseline’s rule calls for (`rff - rff_trac`) for 120 quarters (`ELB_FLOOR_QUARTERS`, 30 years: the whole of a backward-looking solution) and lifted after that, which only the 200-quarter forward-looking runs reach; a floor lasting all 50 years would rule out any cut for half a century, and many of those runs did not solve. The floor binds exactly at the baseline: the Fed can raise the rate but not cut it.
* **Financing.** “Debt rule” is the Board’s own fiscal rule (`dfpdbt = 1`): the personal tax rate rises over time to stabilize the ratio of debt to GDP. “Taxes fixed” switches it off (`dfpex = 1`, with the trend tax rate on its baseline path), so a shock is deficit-financed and nothing pays for it within the horizon.
* **Length.** One quarter (the Fed’s move only), one, two or three years (three: the Fed’s move only), four years (every shock but the Fed’s move), or permanent. Permanent means in force through the end of the solution, 45 or 200 quarters, so a permanent shock never ends inside the ten years shown and forward-looking agents never see it end.
* **Size.** Each shock is stored at −2, −1, 1 and 2 times its unit (the two stock-price shocks also at ±3). Sizes and signs are the engine’s; the page reverses the sign of three shocks so that its slider reads “up” for an increase (taxes, where engine +1 is a cut; the stock-price shocks, where engine +1 is a 10 percent fall; and immigration, where engine +1 is a population 1 percent smaller; `page_sign` in `meta.json`).

**What the page adds, and this package does not rebuild.** The page never runs the model. For a size between the stored ones it scales or interpolates the stored runs of the same sign; it mixes the two business-investment runs for a share of imported equipment between 0 and 100 percent; and when several shocks are combined it adds their stored runs. That arithmetic happens in the page’s JavaScript and is described on the page. This package rebuilds the stored runs it starts from.

## The shocks

The engine’s `apply_shock` puts each shock into a copy of the baseline:

* **`fed`, the federal funds rate:** the rate is set to baseline plus the size, in percentage points, for the length chosen, then the chosen rule takes over. Not built as permanent under forward-looking expectations: with the rate fixed forever nothing pins down expected inflation, and the model’s answer flipped sign with the financing setting.
* **`gov`, government purchases:** federal purchases (`egfe`, taken out of the model) higher by the size times 1 percent of baseline real GDP.
* **`tax`, taxes:** a shift in the personal tax rate equation (`trp`, replaced so the shift is netted out of its own lags and does not compound) worth the size times 1 percent of GDP in revenue; engine +1 is a cut.
* **`transfers`, transfers to households:** the transfer ratio `gtrt` higher by the size times 0.01.
* **`oil`, oil prices:** the price of imported oil (`poil`) higher by the size times $25 a barrel.
* **`dollar`, the dollar:** the real trade-weighted dollar (`fpxr`) higher by the size times 10 percent.
* **`stocks`, stock prices through the required return:** the equity premium (`reqp`) shifted by enough to move stock-market wealth (`wpsn`) by exactly −10 percent times the size on impact under backward-looking expectations. The shift per 10 percent is found by the engine itself (`calibrate_stocks`, stored in `meta.json` as `calibration`) and scaled in logs, so ±3 gives exactly ±30 percent. The required return on equity also sets half of businesses’ cost of capital, so business investment responds directly.
* **`stocks_earn`, stock prices through expected earnings:** the expected growth of real dividends (`zdivgr`, through its add factor) shifted by enough for the same move in wealth (`calibrate_earnings`). Expected dividend growth enters only the stock-wealth equation, so this version works through household wealth alone. Under forward-looking expectations the add factor is set to x(t) − 0.990 x(t+1), because the forward-looking `zdivgr` equation sums a constant add factor over the future; this keeps expected dividend growth x above the model’s own expectation in each quarter the shock is in force. Under forward-looking expectations the realized move in wealth differs from the size chosen (for both stock-price shocks), and the page says so when it is off by half a point or more.
* **`termprem`, long-term rates:** the 10-year term premium (`rg10p`) higher by the size in percentage points.
* **`mfp`, productivity:** for one year, a one-time 1 percent rise in the level of multifactor productivity (`mfpt_aerr`, the Board’s standard supply shock); for longer, trend productivity growth (`hmfpt`) 1 point a year faster for that long.
* **`realloc`, labor market reallocation:** the natural rate of unemployment (`lurnat`) higher by the size in percentage points. FRB/US has no industries, so this is reallocation’s footprint: lower potential output and more wage pressure at a given unemployment rate.
* **`tariff`, tariffs:** a tariff on non-oil goods imports at the size times 10 percent, built by `engine/tariff.py` (`channels`). The duty E is the tariff rate times baseline non-oil goods imports while it is in force: the model’s non-oil imports (`emon`) include services, so E = 0.10 × size × 0.78 × `emon`, where 0.78 (`GOODS_SHARE` in `engine/build.py`) is goods’ share of non-oil imports of goods and services (`SOURCES.md`); for a 10 percent tariff E is about 1.1 percent of GDP at the baseline. It goes to the Treasury as customs receipts (`gfrecn_aerr`, `gfsrpn_aerr`). Consumers pay half of it through consumer prices (a share calibrated so the model’s effect of the 2025–26 tariffs on core PCE inflation matches published estimates; `SOURCES.md`), spread over three quarters (0.25, 0.45, 0.30): the desired consumer price level (`qpcnia`) rises by L = log(1 + 0.5 E / consumer spending) and core inflation (`picxfe`) by enough to get there on that schedule. Businesses buying equipment pay half the duty on capital goods (29 percent of the duties, capital goods’ share of goods imports) through the price of investment, whose equation (`pbfir`) is replaced so the relative price stays on its baseline path apart from that shift. Company profits (`ynicpn_aerr`) absorb the remaining 35.5 percent, so companies bear half in all. The tariff-inclusive price holds imports down a quarter later (`emo_aerr`, through log(1 + E / `emon`), the average duty on all non-oil imports); the import price `pmo` is not moved, because it is measured before duties.
* **`immig`, immigration:** the working-age population (`n16`) smaller by the size times 1 percent (engine +1 = smaller).
* **`expect`, inflation expectations:** long-run expected inflation (`ptr`, taken out of the model) higher by the size in percentage points.
* **`credit`, credit spreads:** the BBB corporate bond risk premium (`rbbbp`) higher by the size in percentage points; it also raises the equity premium.
* **`invest` and `investimp`, business investment:** run on a version of the model in which business investment and non-oil imports are each split into the Board’s own equation (renamed) plus an added piece taken as given (`engine/invest_model.py`): `ebfi = ebfio + edc` and `emo = emoo + emdc`. An add factor on the Board’s equations would be worked off, because each corrects toward a desired level. The added investment `edc` is the size times 1 percent of baseline nominal GDP at baseline investment prices; `emdc` is its imported share at the baseline import price (none for `invest`, all of it for `investimp`). Each added dollar enters GDP at its baseline price (`dcw = 1`), and the added capital is not counted in the capital stock that sets potential output (`kdcsw = 0`): a demand shock. With both pieces zero the split model reproduces the Board’s baseline exactly.

## What did not solve, and what is not built

* **Runs that did not solve.** Every backward-looking run solved. Some forward-looking runs did not converge within 40 iterations, mostly permanent shocks that carry the economy far from its baseline over the 50-year solution (the dollar and productivity growth above all), and some at the lower bound. Each file lists them under `failed`, with the solver’s message, and the page names a combination that did not solve. A slower trust-region solver (`engine/build.py --retry-failed`) rescued only a few of them when it was tried, so they are the model’s limits more than the solver’s.
* **Not built at all.** Forward-looking expectations at the lower bound with the Board’s debt rule: in an early build only 86 of 680 such runs converged, so those files do not exist and the page greys the setting out (`dropped` in `meta.json`). And the permanent Fed move under forward-looking expectations (above).

## What the model leaves out

FRB/US has a flat Phillips curve with well-anchored inflation expectations, so slack moves inflation little; it is close to linear, with no financial accelerator and no bottlenecks, and apart from the funds-rate floor no constraints that bind only sometimes; most household spending follows expected lifetime income; and most spending and hours respond gradually. Anything it does not contain, such as industries, supply chains or a financial crisis, appears only through its footprint on the shocks it has. The simulator page discusses where this probably makes its answers too small.
